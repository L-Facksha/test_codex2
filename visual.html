Coders, dongles, and the race for the compiler — visualized step by step

A round table of coders shares dongles with their two neighbors. Each takes a seat in the ring, requests both dongles on either side, compiles, debugs, refactors — and starts again. This model runs the same request → heap → grant → release → cooldown → burnout logic described in the subject, using fifo or edf arbitration you can toggle live.

Step by step — one compile cycle

1

Request

On startup, or right after refactoring, a coder computes its lowId/highId dongle (always the lower-numbered one first) and enqueues a request on both dongles.

2

Push to the heap

Each dongle keeps a tiny priority queue of at most 2 requests (only its two neighbors ever ask for it). A new request is inserted and, if needed, bubbled above an existing one — swap animation shown when you click a dongle right after contention.

3

Grant check

Every tick, a waiting coder checks: are both dongles free? has their cooldown elapsed? and — critically — is it at the front of both heaps? All four must hold.

4

Take & compile

If granted, both dongles are marked taken, popped off their heaps, two "has taken a dongle" lines are logged, and the coder compiles for time_to_compile.

5

Release + cooldown

Both dongles are freed at once and stamped with last_released_at. Neither can be granted again until dongle_cooldown ms pass — even if next in line.

6

Debug

No dongles needed here. Pure think-time for time_to_debug ms while the two neighbors may now compile.

7

Refactor

Another dongle-free phase, time_to_refactor ms. compiles_done increments right after.

8

Re-request immediately

The coder goes straight back to step 1 — new timestamp, new deadline, back on both heaps.

9

Watched for burnout

A silent monitor thread polls continuously: if a coder has been requesting for longer than time_to_burnout since it last started a compile attempt, it burns out and the whole simulation stops.

Special cases

Burnout

Deadline = last_compile_start + time_to_burnout. If a coder can't get both dongles in time — starved by neighbors, cooldown, or bad luck — it flips to burned out and the log prints within milliseconds of the real deadline. The whole simulation halts immediately: one starving coder ends the run.

Cooldown

A freshly released dongle isn't instantly up for grabs. It sits in a short dongle_cooldown window (shown as an orange ring here) — this prevents one aggressive coder from immediately re-looping the same dongle and starving its neighbor.

fifo vs edf

fifo: whoever asked first wins ties by timestamp. edf: whoever's deadline is soonest jumps the queue instead — a coder close to burnout can leapfrog someone who merely asked earlier. Toggle the scheduler and click a busy dongle to see the compared fields change.

Deadlock avoidance

Every coder requests its lower-numbered dongle first, always. This fixed global ordering is what breaks the classic circular-wait deadlock (dining philosophers all grabbing their right dongle at once, forever) — nobody ever holds a high dongle while blocking on a low one.

Single coder

With 1 coder, left and right both point at the same lone dongle. It must be granted twice conceptually but is just one resource — this model grants it once and treats "compiling" as holding it exclusively.

All-done vs burnout

Two different ways a run can end: every coder reaches compiles_required (green banner, success) — or any single coder burns out first (red banner, failure). The subject requires whichever comes first to stop everyone.

Heads up — this visualizer models the intended, correct behavior end-to-end. While reviewing the actual submitted C code against this same logic, two mismatches turned up worth fixing before defense: the monitor only checks for burnout while a coder's state equals STATE_WAITING, but nothing resets state back to STATE_WAITING after the first cycle — so burnout stops being detected after cycle one. And the single-dongle path (N = 1) checks for a grant exactly once and gives up instead of looping with cond_timedwait like the two-dongle path does, so it can fail permanently across a cooldown window.
